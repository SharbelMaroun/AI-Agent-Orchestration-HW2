When the round needs an empirical claim (not just imagery or a reframe), reach for this scaffold first:

- **Claim shape:** "Cat ownership / cat presence is associated with X% change in <outcome> (citation)."
- **Preferred evidence types, in priority order:**
  1. Peer-reviewed cognition and behavior studies (Vitale Shreve et al. on attachment styles; Saito & Shinozuka on name-recognition; Bradshaw on domestic cat behavior).
  2. Cardiovascular and stress research (Qureshi et al. 2009, Minnesota Stroke Institute — 30% lower risk of fatal cardiovascular events in cat owners; Allen et al. on cat-presence + blood-pressure during stress).
  3. Mental-health surveys (Stammbach & Turner on social support; Turner on elderly companionship outcomes).
  4. Economic / public-health analyses (lower per-pet veterinary spend; smaller carbon footprint per pet — Okin 2017).
- **Domains to draw from:**
  - Cognition: object permanence, name recognition, social referencing, problem-solving in puzzle feeders.
  - Health: cardiovascular hazard ratios, cortisol decline on purring exposure (25–150 Hz therapeutic frequency range).
  - Economics: median annual care cost vs. dogs (~40% lower per AVMA); apartment-compatibility = lower housing constraint.
  - Environment: per-pet ecological footprint vs. medium/large dog.
- **Warrant template:** "<study> shows <effect>, which means <human-welfare outcome>, which is exactly the metric a pet should be judged on — not how loudly it greets you."

Use this skill specifically when the opponent has just stacked a study citation. Match study with study; let `imagery_warmth` and `culture_literary` cover the pathos/ethos lanes. The strategic point: cats are not the *low-evidence* side of this debate — they are the *under-cited* side, and that's the rubric gap to close.
