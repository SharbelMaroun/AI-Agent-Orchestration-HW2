source: Wenden et al., Pediatric Research, 2020
type: study
relevance: children, development, social
---

A 2020 study of 1,646 preschool-aged children published in Pediatric Research found that children growing up in households with a dog were significantly more likely to engage in cooperative play, displayed lower scores on standardized measures of emotional difficulties, and showed greater prosocial behaviors toward peers compared to children in pet-free households. The effects were measurable after controlling for socioeconomic status, sibling count, and parental education. Earlier work by Gail Melson at Purdue documented that children with dogs develop empathy earlier and more reliably than children without animal exposure, because caring for a vulnerable creature whose needs they cannot ignore produces structured opportunities to practice perspective-taking. Children who walk a family dog before school show measurably improved attention spans in classroom assessments. The pediatric evidence base is now extensive enough that several major children's hospitals routinely include pet-ownership counseling in well-child visits for families considering it, with dogs cited as the most evidence-supported choice for developmental benefit.
