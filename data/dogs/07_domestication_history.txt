---
source: Larson et al., PNAS, 2012
type: history
relevance: history, domestication, partnership
---

Dogs were the first species humans domesticated, by a margin of thousands of years. Genetic and archaeological evidence suggests the divergence of dogs from wolves began between 20,000 and 40,000 years ago — predating agriculture, settled villages, pottery, and even the domestication of fire-cooked plants. The 14,000-year-old Bonn-Oberkassel burial in Germany contains a man, a woman, and a domesticated dog interred together with grave goods, evidence that even Paleolithic hunter-gatherers considered dogs family members worth burying with honor. Cats were not domesticated until roughly 9,500 years ago, well after agriculture created the grain stores that attracted rodents. The chronological asymmetry is not trivial: it means dogs and humans have been mutual collaborators across the entire span of human civilization. Every culture that has produced written records has produced records of dogs. The relationship is not an accident of recent affluence; it is older than every city, every nation, every religion that humans have ever built.
