The U.S. Department of Defense currently maintains roughly 1,600 active military working dogs, trained at the 341st Training Squadron at Lackland Air Force Base. Military dogs detect explosives, search buildings, patrol perimeters, and accompany special operations units on missions deemed too dangerous for additional human personnel. A Belgian Malinois named Cairo accompanied the SEAL Team Six raid that killed Osama bin Laden in 2011. A dog named Conan was wounded chasing ISIS leader Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi to his death in 2019. The military estimates that each working dog saves between 150 and 1,800 lives over its career through bomb detection alone. Dogs have served in every U.S. armed conflict since World War I, where 50,000 trained dogs were deployed as messengers, scouts, and sentries. The cultural debt is real: in 2000, Congress passed Robby's Law, ensuring military working dogs retire to adoption rather than being euthanized at end-of-service. No other companion species has been entrusted with comparable responsibility, and no other has earned the kind of formal legislative recognition Congress has granted to military dogs.
