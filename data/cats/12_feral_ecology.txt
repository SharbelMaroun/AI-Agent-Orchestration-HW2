Wherever humans have stored grain, cats have followed, and wherever cats have followed, rodent populations have collapsed. The Roman empire's grain trade with North Africa shipped cats by the boatload. The British Royal Navy maintained ship's cats until 1975 — the rodents that would otherwise eat through provisions, gnaw rope, and chew electrical wiring on long voyages were considered a problem only a cat could solve. Modern estimates suggest that domestic cats save approximately 19 million tons of grain from rodent predation worldwide each year, a quantity that would otherwise represent calories sufficient to feed roughly 40 million people. The Library of Congress, the British Museum, and the Hermitage have all kept cats specifically to protect manuscripts and artwork from rodent damage. The Hermitage cats — currently around fifty of them, with their own staff and a museum-issued passport — have been on continuous duty since Empress Elizabeth ordered them in 1745. No other companion species has performed comparable infrastructure work for civilization, and no other has done it with less direct supervision.
