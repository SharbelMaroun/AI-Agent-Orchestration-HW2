The Maneki-Neko, the beckoning cat statuette with one upraised paw, sits on the entrance counter of nearly every traditional shop, restaurant, and tea house in Japan, and on a growing number across East Asia and the Western world. The figure traces to the seventeenth-century legend of a wandering nobleman who, sheltering from a storm beneath a tree, noticed a cat at a nearby temple beckoning him inside. He followed the cat. A moment later, lightning struck the tree he had been standing under. He patronized the temple for the rest of his life. Whether the legend is literal or constructed afterward, the cultural fact is that an entire economy has, for four centuries, asked a cat for good luck before opening for business. No comparable iconography exists for dogs in any major commercial tradition. The cat became the symbol of fortune because cats, unlike dogs, are believed to enter and depart on their own counsel — and what a shopkeeper most wants is not a loyal servant but a discerning patron who has freely chosen to step through the door.
