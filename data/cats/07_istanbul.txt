Istanbul, the city that has been three empires and many names, is built around an estimated 125,000 street cats who are not feral and not owned. They live in the public spaces of mosques, in bookstores, in restaurants, in the gardens of private homes. Shopkeepers leave bowls of milk. Commuters carry kibble in their pockets. Veterinarians treat strays without payment. The cats are not lost; they have a city. The 2016 Turkish documentary "Kedi" introduced this arrangement to the world, and audiences who had assumed urban living and animal dignity were incompatible were forced to reconsider. "A cat meowing at your feet, looking up at you," one Istanbul resident says in the film, "is life smiling at you." The city does not maintain this relationship out of duty. It does so because the cats add something — call it grace, call it composure — that civic planners cannot legislate and tourists cannot buy. A city's character is partly the character of the creatures it tolerates. Istanbul has chosen well.
