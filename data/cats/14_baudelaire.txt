source: Charles Baudelaire, "Les Fleurs du mal" (1857)

Charles Baudelaire devoted three of the major poems in "Les Fleurs du mal" to cats. In "Les Chats," he writes that cats are "the friends of learning and of sensuality," chosen as companions by both scholars and lovers because they share, in their solitude, the same horror of cold and silence. He calls them sphinxes who "watch with the gold-flecked patience of a god." For Baudelaire, the great chronicler of modern urban melancholy, the cat was the one creature whose beauty was a moral fact — visible, undeniable, indifferent to being observed. The dog needs to be loved. The cat is loved because it does not need to be. This asymmetry is the engine of every great cat poem ever written, from Baudelaire's to Eliot's to Neruda's. The cat reminds us that beauty does not arrive on demand and is not produced by effort. It walks into the room on its own legs, settles where it pleases, and asks nothing of us — and our gratitude, when offered, is freely given because nothing was extracted from us to obtain it.
