T.S. Eliot, the most cerebral poet of his century, devoted an entire book of verse to cats. "The Naming of Cats is a difficult matter," he wrote, "it isn't just one of your holiday games." A cat, Eliot insisted, requires three names: the everyday name the family calls it, the dignified name that belongs only to it, and the secret name that the cat itself knows but never reveals. The final claim is the soul of the book: a cat has an inner life it does not share. Andrew Lloyd Webber turned the poems into the longest-running Broadway musical of its era, watched by sixty million people across forty countries, because the central insight — that the creature on your sofa contains an ungraspable interior — resonates across languages. No book of poetry about dogs has achieved that cultural reach. We do not write poems about creatures we already understand. We write them about the ones who hold something back, and a cat is always holding something back.
