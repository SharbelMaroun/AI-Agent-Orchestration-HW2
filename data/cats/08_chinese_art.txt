Song dynasty painters, working in the eleventh and twelfth centuries when Chinese landscape painting reached its philosophical peak, returned again and again to the cat as a subject. Mao the cat appears in scroll paintings curled beside scholars at their writing desks, watching butterflies, sleeping among chrysanthemums. The poet Lu You, retiring to his garden in the twelfth century, wrote dozens of poems about the cats who shared his exile: "The salt is gone, and I have nothing to repay my cat's vigilance," begins one. Chinese tradition assigned the cat a particular role — companion to the scholar, witness to contemplation, presence that does not interrupt thought. The dog, by contrast, was associated with rural labor and military service. The scholar painting his cat was painting his own ideal: present, attentive, self-contained, unmoved by the noise of the world outside the window. Twelve centuries later, the iconography still works. The image of a thinker and a cat is shorthand for a certain kind of life that cats, more than any other companion, seem to make possible.
