source: Michel de Montaigne, "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (1580)

Michel de Montaigne, the inventor of the personal essay, paused mid-meditation on the limits of human knowledge to ask one of the most quietly devastating questions in philosophy: "When I play with my cat, how do I know that she is not passing time with me rather than I with her?" The line punctures four centuries of Western confidence that humans occupy the privileged vantage point in their relations with animals. Montaigne, holding a piece of string for a cat in his Bordeaux tower, recognized the cat as a creature with her own agenda — her own assessment of the situation, her own decision to engage or withdraw, her own theory of what was worth doing on a slow afternoon. That moment of philosophical vertigo is exactly what cat-keeping offers any reflective person. The cat does not perform for you, does not need you, does not flatter your sense of importance. She invites you to wonder whether your assumptions about your place in the household — and in the universe — are correct.
