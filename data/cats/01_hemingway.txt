Ernest Hemingway, who had crossed bullfighting rings and African plains and the front lines of three wars, kept dozens of cats at his home in Key West and considered them companions worth writing about more often than he wrote about his wives. "A cat has absolute emotional honesty," he wrote. "Human beings, for one reason or another, may hide their feelings, but a cat does not." The descendants of his polydactyl cats — born with six toes per paw, a mutation Hemingway prized — still wander the grounds of the Key West house, all fifty-some of them, protected by the estate and listed by name in his correspondence. When Hemingway wanted to describe what a friend or a lover should be, he reached for the language of cats: independent, dignified, present on their own terms. The man who refused to flinch in front of a dying lion confessed that he could not bear to disappoint a cat. He understood the asymmetry: a cat does not need you, and that is precisely what makes its presence a gift.
