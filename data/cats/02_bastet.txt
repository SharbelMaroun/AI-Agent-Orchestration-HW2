For nearly three millennia, ancient Egyptians worshipped the goddess Bastet, depicted as a woman with the head of a cat, as protector of the home, of childbirth, of music, and of the sun's gentler aspects. The cult center at Bubastis drew annual pilgrimages of half a million people — Herodotus, who saw it, called it the most spectacular religious festival in the known world. Cats lived in temples, were mummified by the millions, and were buried with funerary jewelry. To kill a cat in pharaonic Egypt, even accidentally, was a capital offense; when a Roman soldier killed a cat near Alexandria, the resulting mob violence ended in his execution. Diodorus Siculus, the Greek historian who recorded the incident, noted that no diplomatic appeal from the Roman governor could save the soldier — the crowd would not be calmed. No other domestic animal in human history has been worshipped as a god by an entire civilization for thirty centuries. The cat did not earn this status by usefulness. It earned it by being unforgettable.
