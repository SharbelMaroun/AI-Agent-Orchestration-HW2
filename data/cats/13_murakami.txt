Haruki Murakami has filled his novels with cats: the missing cat in "The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle" whose disappearance unlocks the entire narrative; the wise old cats of "Kafka on the Shore" who can speak with a boy named Nakata; the unnamed black cat of "1Q84." Murakami has said in interviews that he keeps cats because they are the only creatures who consent to share a house with a writer on terms acceptable to both. A dog, he observes, will demand attention and disrupt the silence that fiction requires. A cat will lie on the manuscript, occasionally walking across the typewriter, but otherwise leaves the writer alone to do the work the writer came home to do. The cats in Murakami's fiction often function as portals — through them, characters access the dream-logic that drives his plots. The implication, repeated across thirty years of his work, is that a cat is not merely an animal but an emissary from a quieter, deeper world that we, distracted humans, otherwise have no way to reach.
